17. Planning

17.1. Plan Weekly, Do Daily

This means pinning down what you want to hit, like major goals such as boosting revenue, or the small wins in your daily grind. Without this roadmap, it's easy for your targets to slip into the backseat.

But don't suffocate your days with overplanning. Zoom out and draft a weekly plan instead.

Why? Because each day comes with its own set of surprises, and you need the wiggle room to adapt.

Weekly planning is your buffer zone. It's the strategic way to handle the curveballs life throws without flipping your whole schedule upside down.

Here's what makes planning by the week smarter than a day-to-day hustle:

  • Flexibility: Got a week's worth of hours to toy with? You can pivot without panic. That overrun meeting or last-minute task? Just shuffle your blocks of focused work to another slot in the week.
  • Big-Picture Perspective: A week-wide lens helps you to stitch your tasks into your bigger ambitions. This isn't just a to-do list; it's your strategic ally, nudging every effort a step closer to those end-goals.

But the real secret? It's all about what you do each day. The steady drumbeat of daily action, no matter how small, is what drives your progress forward.

Forget about biting off more than you can chew; it's the daily nibbles that avoid burnout and keep you moving.

Think of your workload as a jigsaw puzzle. It's the tiny pieces that, day by day, add up to the big picture. Instead of lunging at the whole puzzle and ending up stalled, you tackle it piece by piece. And that's how you build a rhythm that can last.

When you combine weekly planning with daily action, you've got a powerhouse combo. It's about laying tracks for the long haul and then putting down the ties, one by one, every single day.

This is how you keep the momentum up and roll your efforts into something big, something that sticks.

Ready to get your planning game on point? Dive into the nitty-gritty of crafting a plan that works: Your Guide to Effective Planning & Scheduling

17.2. Why Schedule? Block Your Calendar For Important Things

Let's get real for a moment: if you don't carve out time for what's important, life will decide for you, and it's not going to be in your favor.

If you want to make things happen, you've got to put them on your calendar like they're appointments you can't miss.

This means earmarking time for the big tasks and the little things, too—yes, that includes time to kick back and take a breather. I talk about creating ‘*Focus Blocks*‘ in the Time Flow System. This is your power move, where you grab your day by the collar and tell it who's boss.

Focus block.jpg ~ cdn.lifehack.org

Open up your calendar and picture your day as a series of blocks, just waiting to be filled with your priorities. Place your tasks into these blocks deliberately, like you're piecing together a puzzle.

Maybe you set aside a block for mapping out your business strategy, another for touching base with clients, and a chunk of time just for sifting through emails.

Remember, these ‘Focus Blocks' don't have to be in hour increments—customize them. Some to-dos might be quick 15-minute bursts, while others might sprawl over a couple of hours.

The key is to fit these blocks to the rhythm of your work and life, not the other way around.

And these blocks, they're more than just placeholders. They should be stepping stones leading you toward your bigger goals — your ‘*North Stars*,' as we explore in the Time Flow System.

When you align your daily blocks with your long-term visions, that's when the magic happens.

17.3. Say No Often

Here's a tough but crucial word to get comfy with: “No.” Not saying it can land you in a heap of trouble.

You might think you're being helpful by taking on every request that comes your way, but there's a cost.

Overcommitting is a surefire way to slow down your work, chip away at the quality, and leave you churning out less-than-your-best. It's better to do a few things well than to do many things poorly.

If you keep saying yes, you'll find yourself with a plate that's not just full—it's spilling over. This isn't just about having too much work; it's about your life tipping out of balance.

Working yourself to the bone isn't a badge of honor; it's a one-way ticket to Burnout City. People will keep piling on the work, oblivious to the fact that you're silently drowning. This isn't just bad for your health; it throws off everything else in your life.

And when you promise the moon and can't deliver, it sets people up to expect the impossible from you. They think you've got it covered when in reality, you're in over your head. This misalignment of expectations isn't good for anyone.

Learning to say “no” is an art—one that will keep you sane and ensure you're only tackling what you can handle while maintaining quality.

The benefits of clocking in.

I found the activity log does not only track the deep and shallow hours, but also proved the Planning Fallacy to me:

The planning fallacy, …, is a phenomenon in which predictions about how much time will be needed to complete a future task display an optimism bias and underestimate the time needed.

I have estimated a task that takes one day but it took me 3 days to finish. Activity log gave me a sense of how my time is spent and how much time do I need for a specific kind of task. Later at night, I can also use this activity log to review what I have done in a day.

18. Prioritisation

Think of prioritization as drawing a treasure map for your day, where X marks the most important tasks. It's the route that gets you to the prize, avoiding those time-consuming detours.

When you set your priorities, you're taking the wheel of your day, steering your time and energy toward what's truly important.

It's like wearing blinders to keep you looking straight ahead—no getting sidetracked by those little, nagging tasks that don't really move the needle.

When everything feels urgent, nothing is. That's why knowing what comes first matters. Without it, you're running in circles, getting busy but not productive, missing the mark on the tasks that count, and that's a one-way ticket to burnout.

Don't just make a to-do list; make a to-do list that makes sense. A list where the big rocks are first, and the sand fills in around them.
